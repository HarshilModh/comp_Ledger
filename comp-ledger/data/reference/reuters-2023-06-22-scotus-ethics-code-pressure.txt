Pressure Mounts on Supreme Court to Adopt Ethics Code
Andrew Chung | Reuters
---
Pressure is mounting on the US Supreme Court to adopt a formal code of ethics following months of revelations about justices accepting undisclosed gifts and luxury travel from wealthy benefactors.

Senate Judiciary Committee Chairman Dick Durbin has scheduled a hearing on Supreme Court ethics reform, with calls growing from both legal scholars and the public for greater transparency and accountability at the nation's highest court.

The push follows a series of reports detailing previously undisclosed relationships between justices and wealthy individuals with potential interests before the court. Justice Clarence Thomas's acceptance of luxury trips from a GOP donor has drawn the most attention, but questions have also been raised about other justices' activities.

Currently, the Supreme Court operates without a binding ethics code, unlike all other federal courts. Justices are expected to follow general ethical principles but face no formal enforcement mechanism and make their own recusal decisions without external review.

"The court has operated on an honor system that is clearly insufficient," said a former federal judge who now teaches at Yale Law School. "The absence of binding rules creates a vacuum that inevitably gets filled by the appearance of impropriety."

The court's defenders argue that imposing external ethics oversight would violate judicial independence and the separation of powers. Some conservative legal scholars say existing mechanisms, including potential impeachment by Congress, provide adequate checks.

Several legislative proposals have been introduced, ranging from requiring the court to adopt a formal code to establishing an inspector general for the judiciary. However, the prospects for legislation remain uncertain given Republican opposition in the House.

Public confidence in the Supreme Court has declined significantly in recent years, according to polls, though analysts debate whether ethics concerns or disagreements over decisions are the primary driver.

Some legal observers note that the justices could preempt congressional action by voluntarily adopting their own code of conduct. Reports suggest internal discussions about such a step have occurred, though no announcement has been made.
