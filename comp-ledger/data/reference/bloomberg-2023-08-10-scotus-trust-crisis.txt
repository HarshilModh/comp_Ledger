Supreme Court Faces Crisis of Public Trust as New Term Approaches
Greg Stohr | Bloomberg
---
The US Supreme Court enters its new term in October facing a crisis of public confidence, with approval ratings near historic lows and ongoing ethics controversies undermining the institution's claims to impartiality.

A Gallup poll released this week found that only 40 percent of Americans approve of the job the Supreme Court is doing, near the all-time low recorded last year following the Dobbs decision that overturned Roe v. Wade. A separate survey found that a majority of Americans believe justices are influenced by their political views rather than the law.

The ethics scandals involving Justices Thomas and Alito have compounded the decline in public trust, providing concrete examples that critics point to as evidence of institutional problems beyond mere disagreement with decisions.

"The court has always had critics who disagreed with its rulings," said a political scientist at the University of Chicago. "What's different now is the sense that the institution itself is compromised -- that the process, not just the outcomes, is tainted."

Court watchers note that several of the upcoming term's cases have political dimensions that will further test public confidence, including cases involving presidential immunity, federal agency authority, and social media regulation.

The justices have taken limited steps to address the crisis, adopting a code of conduct in November that drew mixed reviews. Chief Justice Roberts has made public statements about the importance of judicial independence but has not directly addressed specific ethics allegations.

Some legal scholars argue the court's legitimacy crisis is overstated, noting that public opinion of the court has always fluctuated and that the institution has weathered previous periods of low confidence. Others say the current moment is qualitatively different because it combines ideological concerns with ethics scandals.

Congressional efforts to impose reforms have stalled, with Republicans blocking legislation in the Senate and controlling the House. Some advocates have turned to other approaches, including proposed constitutional amendments to establish term limits for justices.

The business community has watched the developments with concern, as the court's authority depends on voluntary compliance with its rulings by other branches of government and the public.
