COP28 Climate Summit Wrestles Over Fossil Fuel Phase-Out Language
Akshat Rathi and Jennifer A. Dlouhy | Bloomberg
---
Negotiators at the COP28 climate summit in Dubai are locked in contentious debate over whether the final agreement should call for a "phase-out" or "phase-down" of fossil fuels, a linguistic distinction with enormous economic and political implications.

The conference, hosted by the United Arab Emirates and presided over by Sultan Al Jaber, who also heads the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company, has been shadowed from the start by questions about conflicts of interest. Reports that the host country planned to use the summit to pursue oil and gas deals drew sharp criticism from climate advocates.

A coalition of more than 100 countries, including the EU, small island states, and the United States, is pushing for language committing to a phase-out of fossil fuels. Oil-producing nations led by Saudi Arabia have resisted, arguing that the focus should be on reducing emissions rather than targeting specific fuel sources.

"The science is clear that we cannot meet our climate goals without moving away from fossil fuels," said the EU's climate commissioner. "The question is whether we have the political will to say so clearly."

The debate represents the first time a COP summit has directly confronted the future of fossil fuels in its core negotiating text. Previous agreements have addressed coal specifically but avoided comprehensive language about oil and gas.

Climate scientists watching the negotiations expressed frustration at the pace of progress. "We are debating word choices while the planet burns," said a researcher at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research.

The financial stakes are enormous. The global fossil fuel industry generates trillions of dollars in annual revenue and employs millions of people. Producing nations argue that a rapid transition would devastate their economies without adequate financial support.

Developing nations have demanded that any agreement include substantial new climate finance commitments to help them transition away from fossil fuels and adapt to impacts already underway. Current levels of climate finance fall far short of assessed needs.

The summit is expected to produce a "global stocktake" assessing collective progress toward Paris Agreement goals, which most analysts agree has been insufficient.
