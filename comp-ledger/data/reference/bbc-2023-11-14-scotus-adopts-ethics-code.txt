US Supreme Court Adopts Ethics Code Amid Criticism
Anthony Zurcher | BBC News
---
The US Supreme Court has adopted a formal code of conduct for the first time in its history, responding to months of criticism over undisclosed gifts and luxury trips accepted by justices.

The code, signed by all nine justices, establishes ethical guidelines covering gifts, outside activities, financial disclosure, and recusal. However, critics immediately noted that it lacks an enforcement mechanism and largely codifies existing practices rather than imposing new restrictions.

"This is a step in the right direction, but it's a small step," said a judicial ethics expert at the Brennan Center for Justice. "Without enforcement provisions, it remains essentially an honor system."

The code states that justices should avoid the appearance of impropriety and should not accept gifts that would create such an appearance. It also addresses when justices should recuse themselves from cases, though it leaves recusal decisions to individual justices.

Senate Democrats said the code was insufficient. "A code without teeth is just a suggestion," said Senator Sheldon Whitehouse, who has been leading the push for Supreme Court ethics legislation. "We still need binding, enforceable rules with independent oversight."

Republicans praised the court for taking action voluntarily and said it demonstrated that legislative intervention was unnecessary. "The court has addressed the concerns raised by adopting this code," said Senator John Cornyn.

The adoption comes after revelations that Justice Thomas accepted luxury travel from a wealthy donor without disclosure, and that Justice Samuel Alito took a private jet trip with a hedge fund billionaire who had business before the court. Both justices have said they followed existing guidelines.

Legal scholars noted that the code's statement that it represents a "codification" of longstanding principles could be read as an assertion that no new rules were needed -- and that past conduct was already compliant.

The code does not address several specific situations that generated recent controversy, and its application to past conduct is unclear. Critics say it will be tested by how justices interpret its provisions going forward.
